The secret recipe

Four moves. That's the whole job.

Coaches use them. Cooks use them. So does the person who filmed your favourite tutorial.

MOVE 01

Show it

Do the thing where they can actually see your hands. Slower than feels normal.

MOVE 02

Shrink it

Chop the big scary thing into steps small enough to win at.

MOVE 03

Hand it over

Let them try. Let them mess it up. That mess is where the learning actually happens.

MOVE 04

Check it stuck

Ask them to show you. If they can't yet, that's your next step — not their fault.

For the grown-up at the front

Running “Pass the Spark”

45 minutes · Grade 4 · One screen, one clicker, no printing needed.

The one idea

Guiding someone else is not a building you go to or a job title you get at 25. It is something humans have done around fires, in kitchens, on sidelines and on screens for thirty thousand years — and these nine-year-olds are already doing it. The word “teacher” never appears in the deck on purpose: children who think the job lives only in a classroom rule themselves out of it early.

Before you start

  • Open on the title slide with the lights low — the deck is built for a dark room.
  • Have partners pre-assigned for the 30-second challenge (slide 7).
  • Optional: sticky notes and markers, so the wall in slide 8 also exists in the real room.
  • Clear the wall from the last class with the button on slide 8.

Minute by minute

0–3
Open · slide 1“Hands up if somebody showed you how to do something today.” Wait. Every hand goes up. “Then this whole hour is about you.”
3–8
Where did it come from? · slide 2Think 20 seconds, tell a partner, then vote with the four tiles. Reveal the line at the bottom only after every tile has been tapped at least once.
8–16
Spot the guide · slide 3Pairs guess out loud who the guide is before you flip each card. Collect the six role words — Cook, Older Kid, Grower, Guide, Creator, Coach — on the board as you go.
16–24
Across time · slide 4Give each era to a different volunteer to read aloud. Key question between eras: “What changed? What stayed exactly the same?”
24–29
Which one helps? · slide 5Vote by hands first, then tap. Push the winners: why is B better? Steer toward specific, try-it-yourself, and the check-back question.
29–32
The four moves · slide 6Say them together: Show it. Shrink it. Hand it over. Check it stuck. Leave them visible for the next activity.
32–40
The 30-second pass · slide 7Spin a topic. Partner A guides for 30 seconds, then swap. Debrief with one question only: “Which of the four moves did your partner use on you?”
40–44
The wall · slide 8Each child says their sentence aloud; type them in as they go, or hand over the keyboard. Read the finished wall back to the room.
44–45
Close · slide 9Let the sparks light one by one in silence. Last line, then out. Resist explaining it.

Questions worth asking

  • “Who taught the person who taught you?” (Follow the chain back as far as the room can go.)
  • “Has anyone here ever learned something from someone younger than them?”
  • “What is something you know that almost nobody else in this room knows?”
  • “What is the hardest part of explaining something to someone who doesn't get it yet?”

If you have more or less time

  • Short on time: cut slides 4 and 6; keep the 30-second pass, it does the most work.
  • Extra time: after slide 7, run a second round where partners guide someone new — the second telling is always better, and naming why is the whole lesson.
  • Follow-up week: each child prepares a two-minute “pass it on” for a younger class. Real audience, real stakes.

Watching for learning

  • During slide 3, does the child name a role without being prompted with the word?
  • During the 30-second pass, do they break the skill into steps unprompted (Move 02)?
  • Do they check back — “got it?”, “you try” — without you asking them to?
  • Exit line: their sentence on the wall is the evidence. Screenshot it.

Being careful

Some children in the room may not have an adult at home who shows them things. Slide 2 deliberately offers four sources, and any one of them is a full answer — never push a child toward the “home” tile.
